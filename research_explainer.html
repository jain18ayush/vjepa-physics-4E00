V-JEPA 2 on the blue-disk clips, both papers

A circle you can walk, inside a tube you have to steer

Every experiment after the first one was an attempt to say what kind of shape direction is. The shape that survived is a circle with thickness.

  1. Speed and acceleration magnitude are already linear at layer 0, and they live in a handful of dimensions. A straight line steers them. A curve does not help.
  2. Direction is weak at the start and jumps at layer 5. Walking a periodic spline through the angle centroids passes through the angles in between. A straight chord teleports.
  3. That circle is a spine, not the whole representation. About two directions in the tube around it hit a target angle as well as twenty orthogonal probes, and they do it without giving up the ordered walk.

Blue is the 1D concept. Amber is the neighborhood activations actually occupy. Red is what linear steering does on a half turn. The rest of this page is how that picture was earned, in order, and which pieces of it are still guesses.

The world being measured

A frozen video encoder, and three sets of clips that change one physical knob at a time.

V-JEPA 2 is a video world model. Its encoder turns a clip into a latent sequence. During pretraining, a predictor tries to guess the latent of masked parts of the video. Nothing in this project trains that model, and nothing runs the predictor. The object of study is the encoder’s residual stream: the vector sitting after each transformer block.

The checkpoint is facebook/vjepa2-vitl-fpc64-256. ViT-L, 24 blocks, width 1024, patch size 16, tubelet 2, crop 256. The “64” in the name is a pretraining frame count. It does not change the encoder. Every clip here is 16 frames, and those 16 frames are what gets fed in. Repeating frames to fake a longer clip would destroy speed and acceleration.

Each clip is a blue disk on a dark background, 256×256, 24 fps. Three datasets, generated separately:

What comes out of one forward pass

Sixteen frames, tubelet 2, so 8 temporal steps. A 16-pixel patch on a 256 image is a 16×16 grid. That is 8 × 16 × 16 = 2048 tokens, each of size 1024. hidden_states[0] is the patch embedding. hidden_states[1 + ℓ] is the residual after block ℓ, for ℓ from 0 to 23. The paper’s layer index is ℓ, the block, not the tuple index.

Every later experiment uses the mean of those 2048 tokens. One vector per layer per clip. The saved tensor is (N, 24, 1024). This is the paper’s primary probe input: linear probes on mean-pooled space–time patches. A last-tubelet pooling ablation was planned only if direction was already strong at layer 0. It was not, so the rest of the pipeline stays on the mean.

Two papers, two questions

Part 1 asks whether physics is in the latent. Part 2 asks what shape that latent has, because the shape is what you steer along.

Joseph et al., the physics paper

Interpreting Physics in Video World Models, arXiv:2602.07050. They probe video world models, including V-JEPA 2-L, on rendered balls. Three claims this project tried to reproduce, qualitatively, on a simpler disk:

Speed and acceleration magnitude are linearly readable from the early layers. Direction becomes readable only around a third of the way through the network — their Physics Emergence Zone, layer 8 of 24 on V-JEPA 2-L. Direction is stored redundantly, on the order of 40–50 features, with a sawtooth as you delete them. Speed is lower-dimensional and decays smoothly. Editing one probe axis barely moves decoded angle. Coordinating about 20 orthogonal probes, on held-out clips, drops error to a fixed target from roughly 83° to roughly 12°.

The method lives in Appendix B (layer probes), C.11 (iterative nullspace), and C.12 (multi-probe steering). Sibling page: the Part 1 method sketch.

Wurgaft et al., the manifold paper

Manifold Steering Reveals the Shared Geometry of Neural Network Representation and Behavior, arXiv:2605.05115, from Goodfire. They argue that concepts in a network form curves, and that steering should walk the curve rather than cut the chord. On a circle, the chord crosses the interior and the model’s outputs jump to a mixture of the endpoints. The spline passes through neighboring concepts.

Language models have a distribution over tokens, so they can build a second curve in behavior space and test whether the two curves are isometric. V-JEPA has no concept vocabulary. The relevant precedent in their paper is mountain car: a small visual world model, a curve through encoder states parameterized by position, and a manufactured “belief” given by softmax over distance to those centroids. Sibling page: the spline method sketch.

The handoff between the papers is the interesting part. Joseph says direction is a high-dimensional subspace you edit with many probes. Goodfire says a concept is a 1D curve. Both can be true if the extra dimensions are thickness around the curve rather than extra concepts. That sentence is the hypothesis the later experiments were built to test. It was not assumed at the start.

Terms, before the plots

The same few objects get reused under new names. This is the short list. The glossary at the bottom is the long one.

\[ \hat{y} = Wh + b \]

A probe is that linear map. \(h\) is the pooled residual, 1024 numbers. For speed and acceleration, \(y\) is the scalar in m/s or m/s². For direction, \(y\) is the pair \((\sin\theta, \cos\theta)\), and the loss is mean squared error on that pair. Training on the wrapped angle is a different experiment and was not done. Sine and cosine are smooth at 0°/360°; a raw angle is not.

\[ \mathrm{err} = \big((\hat\theta - \theta + 180^\circ) \bmod 360^\circ\big) - 180^\circ \]

Circular MAE is the mean of the absolute wrapped error, in degrees. Predicting atan2 of the sine/cosine pair gives \(\hat\theta\). Chance, if you guess randomly on a uniform circle, is 90°. An R² of 1 means the probe matches the labels. An R² of 0 means it does no better than predicting the training mean. Negative R² means it does worse than that mean.

A centroid is the average activation of every training clip with one exact label: one point per angle, or per speed. A spline is a smooth curve through those centroids, in order. The chord is the straight line between two centroids. The spine is the spline, once the later experiments have shown that activations do not sit on it — they sit around it. The tube is that neighborhood, measured perpendicular to the spline’s tangent.

Where each variable becomes readable

If direction only appears halfway through the network, that is where a steering experiment should intervene. The layer sweep exists to find that place, not to decorate a plot.

At every layer, a linear probe is trained with Adam for 100 epochs, full batch, MSE. The paper’s 20-pair sweep over learning rate and weight decay is run inside each fold. Scores are 5-fold GroupKFold on train+val only. The layer itself is chosen on a separate refit: train on the whole train split, score on val. Test clips never enter this choice.

The selection rule, applied to velocity-only direction, because the mixed direction set blurs the rise:

\[ L_{\mathrm{emerg}} = \min\left\{\ell : R^2(\ell) \ge \tfrac{1}{2}\big(R^2(0) + R^2_{\max}\big)\right\} \]

Use \(L_{\mathrm{emerg}}\) as \(L^\star\) if speed and acceleration are both at least R² 0.5 there. Otherwise use the peak. On this data \(R^2(0) = 0.23\), the max on the val table is 0.97 at layer 23, the midpoint threshold is crossed at layer 5, and both scalars are above 0.5 there (0.95 and 0.92). So \(L^\star = 5\). The paper’s layer on Kubric balls was 8. The rule was followed; 8 was not hard-coded.

The temporal-last pooling check was specified to run only if velocity direction was already above R² 0.5 at layer 0. It was 0.23, so that ablation never ran.

What this forced next

Scalars are available immediately and stay available. Direction is the variable with a location. Every causal experiment after this one is run at layer 5, because that is the first layer where the direction probe has crossed the midpoint of its rise and the scalars are already reliable. Layer 23 being slightly better at reading direction is noted, and then deliberately not used: the comparison with the paper is about the emergence layer, and a late layer is a different object (the tube-why filmstrip comes back to this).

How many dimensions, once you can read it

A single probe direction can be a fluke, or one copy of a feature the network stores many times. Iterative nullspace probing deletes the probe and asks whether the label is still there.

At layer 5 only, with a fixed recipe from Appendix C.11: Adam, learning rate \(10^{-3}\), weight decay \(10^{-4}\), 100 epochs for direction and 50 for the scalars. No learning-rate sweep. That is why the first direction probe here has val R² about 0.75, lower than the 0.93 from the swept layer curve. Different training recipe, same activations.

\[ X \leftarrow X - X Q Q^{\top} \]

\(Q\) is a reduced QR of the probe weight, transposed so the columns span the rows of \(W\). Direction’s \(W\) is \(2 \times 1024\), so each kept probe removes up to two dimensions. A scalar probe removes one. The bias is used for prediction and is not part of the subspace. Validation activations are projected with the same \(Q\). The score recorded for probe \(k\) is the score before that probe’s direction is removed.

Stop, on val, from C.11 rather than the looser cutoffs in the paper’s figure caption. Direction: R² under 0.1, or circular MAE over 80°. Scalars: R² under 0.05, or MAE over 0.9 times the error of predicting the training-set mean. The probe that crosses the threshold is not kept and is not used for steering.

The direction curve is jagged: it drops, then partially recovers, repeatedly, including a late spike back to R² 0.57 at iteration 24. The paper reads that pattern as sine/cosine partners — delete one axis of a pair and the next probe finds the other. On this run the jag is real and irregular, not a perfect every-other-step tooth. Speed is not that pattern, but it is also not a monotone slide: R² goes 0.88, 0.41, 0.77, 0.54, 0.54, then 0.13 at the stopped probe. One deletion hurts, another direction still carries speed, and then it runs out. Calling speed “smooth” is the comparison with the direction sawtooth, not a claim that the five steps are monotone.

Dimensionality here is rule-relative. The paper’s display of speed ran for ~25 probes under a looser cutoff. Our quoted “5” is the C.11 stop. The stable comparison, which does not depend on the exact cutoff, is direction ≫ speed ≫ acceleration.

What this forced next

Eighty-two dimensions of direction can mean eighty-two different concepts, or many copies of one angle. The scalars being tiny makes the contrast sharp: if a variable is basically one direction in activation space, you do not need a curve to steer it. Direction might. The steering experiment asks whether those copies are causal. The spline experiment asks whether they are copies of a circle.

Whether those dimensions control the readout

A probe can correlate with angle without being a lever. Steering edits the activation and asks a second probe, one that did not build the edit, what angle it now sees.

Only direction is steered in Part 1. The basis is the 41 velocity-subset probes from the nullspace run, fit on train. An evaluation probe is fit from scratch on unsteered test activations, with the same C.11 recipe, and then frozen. It never sees a steered vector. On this data that probe has R² 0.71 and circular MAE 19.2°. The paper’s eval probe was R² 0.99, on 8 angles rather than 64. Weaker is expected. The causal check does not require it to be perfect.

Target angle is 90° for every test clip, matching the paper. For a prefix of \(n\) probes, stack their weights, take a reduced QR to get an orthonormal \(V\), and replace coordinates inside that subspace:

\[ x_{\perp} = x - V V^{\top} x, \qquad x^{\star} = V c^{\star} + x_{\perp} \]

\(c^{\star}\) is the least-squares solution of “every included probe should output \((\sin 90^\circ, \cos 90^\circ)\),” accounting for each probe’s bias and for \(x_{\perp}\). The orthogonal complement of \(V\) is left alone. After the full edit, the steering probes themselves land on that target to numerical noise (MSE about \(10^{-13}\)). The evaluation probe is a different readout and is allowed to miss.

The signature the paper asked for is the opposing pair of curves. As \(n\) grows, error to 90° falls and error to the clip’s real angle rises. At \(n = 1\), target error is still 58°. At \(n = 5\) it is already 11°, and at \(n = 20\) it is 6.3° (4.4° with all 41). The paper needed about 20 probes to reach ~12°. We get there sooner. Plausible reasons: an earlier layer, a visually simpler disk, 64 linearly separable angles, and a large basis waiting to be used. Velocity-only and accelerating test clips move together, so the edit transfers to motion the basis was not fit on.

One subtlety in the table: \(n = 15\) is slightly worse than \(n = 10\) (12.8° versus 10.8°). More probes are not a monotone improvement. The subspace is redundant, and a bad prefix can point the least-squares solve somewhere the eval probe does not fully follow. The trend is the result. The non-monotonic wiggle is also real.

What this forced next

Part 1 is a successful reproduction of the three qualitative claims, with an earlier emergence layer, little late-layer softening, and easier steering. It leaves the geometry unspecified. You know you need a subspace. You do not know whether that subspace is a curve, a ball, or a tube. Part 2 is the Goodfire question asked at layer 5, on the activations Part 1 already saved: if you walk a spline through the centroids, does the probe pass through the intermediate labels, and does that walk replace the 20-probe edit?

Whether direction is a curve

Goodfire’s claim, translated: if the concept is one-dimensional, the spline and the chord share endpoints and disagree in the middle, and only the spline’s middle is a meaningful label.

No new encoder pass. PCA is fit on train activations at layer 5, 64 components, about 98% of the variance for all three variables. That matches their Appendix A.3. One centroid per exact label — 64 of them, no re-binning. A cubic spline through the centroids, one spline per PCA coordinate. Speed and acceleration use a natural cubic spline (second derivative zero at the ends). Direction uses a periodic cubic spline, so 0° and 360° are the same point and the slope matches. The fit interpolates. There is no smoothing penalty.

Direction’s spline is fit on train / velocity only, so it lines up with the Part 1 steering basis. Accelerating clips are scored later as a transfer check.

A path has 21 waypoints, \(t\) from 0 to 1. The intrinsic coordinate \(u\) moves monotonically. For direction, the walk takes the shorter arc. At each waypoint the top-64 PCA block of a test activation is replaced by the path point, and the clip’s leftover coordinates — everything PCA did not keep — stay as they were. That is their Appendix A.6: edit the subspace the manifold lives in, leave the rest of the vector alone. The chord is the same endpoints, linearly interpolated in that same PCA subspace, so the comparison is path shape and not “which coordinates you were allowed to touch.”

\[ h_{\mathrm{chord}}(t) = (1-t)\, s(u_0) + t\, s(u_1) \]
\[ h_{\mathrm{spline}}(t) = s\big((1-t)\, u_0 + t\, u_1\big) \]

The readout is a fresh evaluation probe, fit on unsteered test activations and frozen. It is applied to every waypoint. It is not refit on steered vectors.

Thickness alone does not mean “you need a spline.” For speed, the thickness is not organized as curvature the probe cares about. For direction, the same thickness is organized around a circle the probe does walk. The next two experiments separate those cases.

What this forced next

Direction has a usable circular spine, and a residual that multi-probe can still exploit at a hard endpoint. Two follow-ups fall out of that sentence. Pullback asks: if you optimize a path purely so the probe tracks the angle ramp, do you fall back onto the spline? Tube-not-curve asks: if you keep the spline and add a few directions perpendicular to it, do you close the 18.6° versus 8.5° gap without recreating the chord’s teleport?

Whether the spline is the probe’s preferred path

In the Goodfire paper, you initialize on the chord and optimize the path until behavior tracks the concept. The optimized path falls back onto the activation curve. That is their evidence the two manifolds match. Here the same move was used as a test, not as a confirmation.

Initialize on the chord. Run L-BFGS on a path in the first 32 principal components so the frozen probe’s decoded angle tracks the claimed ramp. Then measure distance, in PCA space, from the optimized path to the spline \(M_h\).

On mountain car, their pullback sits much closer to the activation curve than the chord it started from (0.29 versus 2.22 in their units). On this direction half-turn, the numbers go the other way.

How to read a negative recovery

The circle is a good skeleton for an ordered walk. It is not the unique set of activations that decode as a given angle. Many off-spine offsets still mean “this θ.” An optimizer that only cares about the probe will use those offsets, because they are shortcuts to a lower loss.

That is the definition of a tube, stated in the language of the optimization: the fiber of the direction map is thick. Pullback leaving \(M_h\) is the result, not a failed reproduction of mountain car. The encoder is a different geometry.

Absolute degrees in this table come from the pullback script’s probe. They do not match the 5.5° / 18.6° pair from the earlier spline script. Same comparison, different frozen probe. The section on reading numbers is about that.

What this forced next

If off-spine points can still be the right angle, the endpoint gap might be closable without abandoning the spine. Pin the path to the spline, and spend a few coordinates in the perpendicular residue. If two of those coordinates match twenty probes, the Part 1 subspace and the Part 2 curve were descriptions of one object.

Adding the tube to the walk

Does a small normal bundle close the endpoint gap, while the middle of the path stays on the claimed angle?

For each training velocity activation, find the nearest point on the direction spline, take the residual, and remove the component along the tangent \(T \propto s'(u)\):

\[ r_{\perp} = r - (r \cdot T)\, T \]

PCA on those perpendicular residuals is the tube basis \(V_k\). A second basis takes the Part 1 nullspace probe rows, orthonormalizes them, and projects them orthogonal to the tangent at the target. Steering at waypoint \(t\) is

\[ z(t) = s(u(t)) + t \cdot V_k c^{\star} \]

\(c^{\star}\) is the same least-squares idea as Part 1, now in the tube basis, with the point pinned to the spine. The factor of \(t\) ramps the tube in, so the middle of the path is still mostly the spline. Leftover coordinates outside the 64-D PCA block stay per clip.

The residual is almost entirely normal. Mean tangential energy is 0.03. Mean perpendicular energy is 26.45. Five tube components explain 64% of that residue; twenty explain 91%. The tube is real, and it is moderately low-rank. It is not a full 82-dimensional ball, and it is not a hairline curve.

Final and mid-path errors are the tube script’s held-out probe on 0° to 180°. Multi-probe rows have no mid-path, because that edit aims at the endpoint rather than walking a ramp.

So the practical recipe on this data is: walk the periodic spline when you care about intermediate angles, and add about two normal coordinates when you care about hitting the endpoint. The 82-dimensional Part 1 edit and the 1D Part 2 walk were two slices of that recipe.

What this forced next

The tube works as a control surface. It does not say what the thickness is made of. A fiber of the angle map, a bundle of other physical variables, redundant copies of the probe, an uncertainty radius, or estimation noise would all look like “perpendicular energy” in the construction above. The five-step campaign is the attempt to pull those apart.

What the thickness is made of

Five hypotheses, run on the same spine. The score at the end is mixed on purpose: the tube is not one thing.

A fiber, mostly

Stand at an anchor near 90° and step a unit vector. Read the frozen probe. If the tube is a fiber of the direction map, steps along the tube should barely change θ, and steps along the tangent should change it a lot.

Degrees of decoded angle per unit step. The tangent is about four times more sensitive than tube PC1. PC2 is almost flat. PC1 still leaks about 2° per unit, so the fiber is relative, not perfect.

And a nuisance bundle, especially speed

Cross-validated ridge regression. The spine’s features are \((\sin u, \cos u)\). The tube’s features are the first 10 residual principal components. This R² is not the 1024-dimensional probe R² from Part 1. A spine-only R² of 0.55 does not mean direction got worse. It means the scalar coordinate along the spline, by itself, is a weaker feature than the full residual.

Blue bars: the spine coordinate. Brown bars: ten tube components. The spine carries angle and fails at speed. The tube fails at angle (negative R²) and carries speed, start-\(y\), and motion type.

Regressing covariates out of the activations and refitting the circle: removing start position leaves 97% of the perpendicular energy. Removing speed leaves 72%. Removing both leaves 70%. Position is not the tube. Speed is a real piece of it, about 28% of the energy, and most of the thickness remains after speed is gone.

Copies, but not the top variance axes

Project the Part 1 nullspace probe planes into PCA and compare them to the tube basis. The first five probe planes sit mostly inside a 20-dimensional tube subspace (overlap about 0.71 to 0.89). Variance-ordered prefixes of that tube do not capture probe energy early: mean squared overlap of the first 20 probes is 0.003, 0.007, and 0.05 at k = 1, 2, 5, then 0.29 at k = 10 and 0.45 at k = 20.

This is why both edits worked in the tube experiment even though they are different slices. Residual-PCA k = 2 and nullspace-tube k = 1 both live in the tubular neighborhood. They are not the same coordinates. The directions that steer are not the directions of largest leftover variance. Largest variance, from the nuisance audit, is where speed sits. Steering-relevant coordinates and speed-relevant coordinates come apart. That split is the sharpest unused fact in the campaign.

Not an uncertainty dial

Tube radius \(\rho = \|r_{\perp}\|\) correlates −0.30 with probe confidence \(\|\widehat{\sin}, \widehat{\cos}\|\), +0.29 with angular error, −0.18 with belief entropy, and +0.41 with belief-mode error. Larger radius, slightly worse direction readout. The links are weak. The tube is not primarily a confidence knob, and it is not estimation fluff: it steers, it has stable energy, and it has the depth structure below.

The sentence the campaign supports

The concept is still a circle. Activations occupy a tubular neighborhood whose coordinates are roughly angle, then speed and motion type, then a residual fiber of copies. Multi-probe worked because it searched that neighborhood until the readout saturated, not because angle is 82 independent concepts. The spline walked the middle because order lives on the tangent. Pullback left the spline because probe loss can be improved in the fiber. Two well-chosen normals match a 20-probe edit because you can pin the spine and stop searching.

Choices that change the meaning of a result

These are the decisions most likely to be invisible if you only look at the figures. Each one was a reason a later comparison is fair, or a reason it is not.

ChoiceWhy it was madeWhat would have gone wrong otherwise
Sine and cosine, MSE, then atan2 Appendix C.11 and the dataset note. The circle has no cut. Regressing the raw angle treats 1° and 359° as far apart. A good model of heading looks like a bad R².
Mean over all 2048 tokens The paper’s stated primary probe. Last-tubelet pooling was a conditional ablation and the condition (direction already strong at layer 0) was false. An independent reproduction only saw a layer-8 onset with last-tubelet pooling. Switching silently would have made \(L^\star\) incomparable with the plan.
\(L^\star\) from the velocity midpoint rule, not “use layer 8” The dataset is not Kubric. The rule is the paper’s idea of emergence, applied locally. Hard-coding 8 would have steered a layer past the jump, and would have hidden that this world is earlier.
C.11 stop thresholds, not the figure-caption cutoffs The appendix is the method. The caption is a display. Speed’s “5 dimensions” would have been a larger number. The direction-versus-scalar gap would still be there; the integer would not.
Swept hyperparameters for layer curves, fixed hyperparameters for nullspace and steering That is the split between Appendix B and Appendix C.11. They were not mixed. You cannot compare a nullspace R² to a layer-curve R² as if they were the same probe.
Evaluation probe fit on unsteered data only The readout has to be a witness, not a participant in the edit. A probe fit on steered vectors can agree with the intervention by construction.
Direction spline on velocity clips only Match the Part 1 steering basis. Accelerating clips become a transfer test. A mixed fit would confound “the circle of heading” with “the circle of heading-while-accelerating,” which the tube later shows is a different coordinate.
Chord drawn in the same 64-D PCA block Isolate path shape. Their language-model linear steer replaces the whole residual. Doing that here would mix “straight versus curved” with “edit 64 coordinates versus edit 1024.”
Shorter arc on the circle A half turn is where a chord must cross the interior. A short arc across 0° is where a non-periodic spline would cheat by going the long way. One path cannot show both the teleport and the boundary condition.
No predictor, no pixel edit, no new videos The claim is about the pooled residual and a probe. Pixel-space steering is a different paper. A generated frame that “looks faster” would be a new evaluation, not a stronger version of this one.

The same phrase, three different degrees

If you line the writeups up, “final error of the direction spline” is 18.6° in one place and 19.8° in another, and the spline’s own final error inside the pullback run is 8.8°. Those are not three discoveries.

Each script fits its own evaluation probe on unsteered test activations and then freezes it. Part 1’s steering probe, Part 2’s spline probe, and the pullback/tube probe are different linear maps. They agree on orderings: chord teleports, spline walks, two tube directions beat a large multi-probe edit, pullback leaves the curve and improves the probe. They do not agree on the absolute degree, because a weaker probe starts farther from the truth and a stronger one is easier to satisfy.

A second trap: R² on the full 1024-dimensional residual (0.93 for velocity direction at layer 5, swept probe) is not R² of a ridge on the two numbers \((\sin u, \cos u)\) (0.55 in the nuisance audit). The first says the residual contains angle. The second says the spline coordinate, used as a tiny feature, is only part of how angle is read out. Tube-only R² going negative means those ten components are worse than predicting the mean, which is the fiber claim, not a broken probe.

A third: PCA keeping 98% of variance does not mean the manifold is 64-dimensional. Sixty-four was a budget copied from the Goodfire appendix so the spline would have room. Speed’s Part 1 nullspace says the linearly readable part is about five dimensions. The other components are there; the probe does not need them to walk speed in a straight line.

What the results leave open

Each of these is a fork the existing numbers do not settle. The useful next experiment is the one that would come out differently under two stories you cannot yet tell apart.

Are the steering normals and the speed normals the same directions?

The tube-why campaign says no, indirectly: leading residual PCs predict speed and do not predict angle, while nullspace probes steer angle and are not aligned with those leading PCs. k = 1 of the nullspace tube already hits 2.6°, and k = 1 of variance PCA does nothing (19.7°). That is suggestive and it is not a direct measurement. Take the tube basis ordered by variance, and the tube basis ordered by overlap with the Part 1 probes. For each, regress speed, and steer the endpoint. If the speed axis and the steering axis are orthogonal, factored control is available: you can change heading without changing decoded speed. If they are the same axis, every precise angle edit is also a speed edit, and the right object is a cylinder in \((\theta, \text{speed})\), which is the thin-plate / 2D manifold the Goodfire paper already knows how to fit and this project deliberately skipped.

After you remove speed, does the endpoint still need the tube?

Residualizing speed removed 28% of perpendicular energy and left 72%. Steer the spine-plus-tube recipe on those residualized activations. If k = 0 then matches the old k = 2 result, the endpoint gap was the speed entanglement and the “copies” story was overstated. If you still need about two normals, those normals are a real fiber of angle, and speed was a passenger. Both outcomes are compatible with everything measured so far.

Is the late network a fatter copy of layer 5, or a different entanglement?

From layer 5 to 23, perpendicular energy goes from 26 to 221 and tangent sensitivity falls from 8.8° to 0.8° per unit step, while linear R² stays high. Repeat the k = 2 steer at layers 12 and 23. If two normals still match a large multi-probe edit, depth only adds redundant fiber and layer 5 remains the right control point. If the k you need grows with depth, later layers mix in factors the layer-5 tube does not contain, and the missing late-layer softening in Part 1 was pooling over a reorganization rather than an absence of one.

Constrain pullback to the tube, and see if it still wants to leave

Unconstrained pullback walked away from the spline and got to about 1° of probe error. That might be the tube, or it might be some third region of activation space. Re-run L-BFGS with the path restricted to the spine plus k normals, for k = 2 and k = 10. If k = 2 already matches unconstrained pullback, the off-manifold optimum was the tube you can name. If the unconstrained path still wins by a lot, there is geometry neither the spline nor the residual PCA has captured.

One circle, or two, for the two motion types?

The velocity-fit spline transfers to accelerating test clips (final error 18.4° versus 18.6° on the mixed test set, in the spline script). Motion type is also classified at 0.78 accuracy from the tube. Those can both be true if accelerating clips sit on a parallel circle — same angles, shifted in the normal bundle — or on the same circle with a label the probe ignores. Fit two periodic splines, one per motion type, and measure the distance between them along the tube. A constant offset that lines up with tube PC-speed means direction-and-motion is a cylinder. Overlapping splines mean motion type is written somewhere the angle curve does not see.

Is the scalar endpoint miss the probe, or the geometry?

Speed’s path reaches the centroid and the global probe reads 2.86 instead of 3.52. Acceleration’s Part 2 probe is weak (R² 0.33) under the fixed C.11 recipe, while the swept Part 1 probe at the same layer was 0.92 on val. Before inventing a curved model for either scalar, swap in the swept probe and, separately, score Goodfire’s belief: softmax over squared distance to centroids, temperature 0.5. If belief tracks the ramp and the linear probe does not, the failure is the readout. If both miss, the centroid really is not the magnitude you think it is.

The isometry test was never run

Goodfire’s first check, before pullback, is whether arc length along the activation spline matches arc length along the behavior curve. Chord length should match much less, and on a folded loop it can collapse. Here behavior can be the probe angle, or the belief over the 64 bins. Correlate those arc lengths on the direction spline. A high correlation says the circle is not just a convenient interpolant but a shared geometry in their sense. A low one, despite the clean mid-path walk, says the probe is locally readable without the manifold being isometric — a weaker claim than the paper’s, and worth knowing before you cite them.

What this dataset cannot answer

One blue disk, no occlusion, no texture, no contact. Joseph et al. also run patch-level probes, attentive pooling, and IntPhys-style violation tests. None of that was in scope, and a flat disk will not grow those results by being reread. The emergence layer moved from 8 to 5 relative to their Kubric balls; whether that move is “simpler pixels” or “64 angles instead of 8” is not identified. A follow-up that changes the stimulus is a new project. A follow-up that separates speed from the steering fiber, on these same activations, is the one the current evidence is already pointing at.
